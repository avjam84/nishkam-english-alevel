Virtues & Golden Threads

The students will explore the role of each of the virtues in each of their set texts. They are encouraged to utilise the rich language of the virtues when discussing the actions of the characters and, due directly to how embedded their understanding of the virtues is by this stage of their education, they can fluently analyse the motivations and behaviours of fictional characters through this vital lens.

Applies to every unit this year (as in the map).

Links to other learning

At Key Stage 3 and 4, students have studied poetry, Shakespeare, challenging novels, 19th Century texts and unseen poetry texts to ensure they have had some experience of the requirements and content of the ‘A’ Level Edexcel specification. At Primary level, the students were exposed to texts that directly prepared them for the key themes and ideas present in these carefully-selected set texts. The journey through English at Nishkam is complete by the end of Sixth Form, preparing any student who wishes to pursue a university degree or relevant career with the tools and knowledge to become a great success.

  • ‘A Thousand Splendid Suns’ (Teacher 1) and ‘A Streetcar Named Desire’ (Teacher 2)

On the surface, these two texts are starkly different. Two completely different cultures, two different time periods and two vastly different protagonists. But, at the heart of both texts lies a deep sense of loss and a longing for ‘something else’ that is an ever-present in great literature. Students must study two whole novels and two whole plays. These are the first of each.

Unit 3 in map orderYear 12

‘Wuthering Heights’ (Teacher 1) and ‘The Handmaid’s Tale’ (Teacher 2)

  • ‘Wuthering Heights’ (Teacher 1) and ‘The Handmaid’s Tale’ (Teacher 2)

In their study of Wuthering Heights, students explore complex themes of love, revenge, social class, and the destructive nature of obsession. They examine how Emily Brontë uses Gothic elements – a genre students will be familiar with from their study at KS3 - and a multi-layered narrative to depict intense human emotions and the consequences of generational conflict. At the heart of the novel lies the turbulent relationship between Heathcliff and Catherine, highlighting the struggle between passion and societal expectations.

Students will also compare Wuthering Heights with A Thousand Splendid Suns by Khaled Hosseini. Both novels explore themes of love, suffering, endurance, and the impact of social constraints - particularly on women. Through this comparison, students gain insight into how different cultures and historical contexts shape similar human experiences. Students have previously studied Historical Fiction texts, such as ‘The Bone Sparrow’, ‘Jekyll and Hyde’ and ‘An Inspector Calls’ - they will use this knowledge to delve into the writer’s intentions and big ideas about our world today.

As part of their A-Level coursework, students study Margaret Atwood’s The Handmaid’s Tale, a powerful dystopian novel that explores themes of power, control, gender oppression, and resistance. Through Atwood’s chilling vision of a theocratic regime, students examine how language, politics, and ideology can be used to suppress individual freedom and identity. The novel invites rich discussion around autonomy, surveillance, and the role of women in society.

For their coursework, students compare The Handmaid’s Tale with another dystopian novel of their choice, developing independent research and comparative analysis skills. This builds on their earlier exploration of dystopian fiction in Year 8 through an abridged version of Frankenstein, and later during GCSE with the study of Jekyll and Hyde, both of which introduced them to themes of duality, science, and societal fear.

Unit 4 in map orderYear 12

Unseen Poetry

Unseen Poetry Students continue to develop the skills required at GCSE. Through the delivery of an expert methodology of analysis, students learn how to approach any poetic text, looking for important themes and imagery, as well as the more complex rhythms and patterns of poetry. Meanwhile, the students begin their coursework unit by studying ‘The Handmaid’s Tale’ together as a class before selecting a text of their own to compare directly with this novel. The freedom of choice for this second text offers an exciting opportunity for lovers of literature to truly explore an inspiring novel of their own volition.

Revision – essay writing skills

Revision – essay writing skills

In the final term, students consolidate their learning across all set texts and refine their critical and comparative essay writing skills in preparation for end-of-year examinations. They revisit key texts – Wuthering Heights, The Handmaid’s Tale, A Thousand Splendid Suns, and A Streetcar Named Desire – deepening their understanding of core themes such as power, identity, loss, love, and societal constraints. These are explored through diverse genres and historical contexts, allowing students to make sophisticated cross-textual connections.

Students also continue their study of poetry through the Edexcel Anthology, engaging with both Romantic and Contemporary poems. With Teacher 1 guiding them through contemporary voices such as Burnside and Thorpe, and Teacher 2 focusing on the Romantic works of Wordsworth and Keats, students revisit universal themes of nature, emotion, memory, and human experience. These texts form a strong foundation for analytical comparison, critical thinking, and precise written expression.

Unseen Poetry remains a key component of their development, where students practise a structured and confident approach to analysing unfamiliar poems. This ensures they are equipped with the tools to interpret language, form and structure independently.

Throughout this term, essay writing is at the forefront. Students refine their ability to construct clear, insightful, and well-supported arguments, drawing on a broad range of texts. Dedicated time is also given to coursework, where students compare The Handmaid’s Tale with a dystopian novel of their own choice—offering them the opportunity to develop original lines of inquiry and engage in independent literary research.

By the end of the year, students will have developed not only a deep understanding of the literature studied but also the academic confidence and critical voice essential for success in their final exams and beyond.
